Fill in the brief and convene. The Quality Analyst and the Value Analyst write blind to each other from different slices of the filings. The Risk Officer, the Macro Strategist and the Skeptic attack their lists with the person’s limits, the market backdrop and the record. The analysts rebut name by name. Two Chairs draft competing portfolios, each stress-tested on real prices; the seats choose one, vote on it, and dissent forces a revision. A full session takes four to five minutes.

How this works, and what it is not

The candidates are every scored stock on the Bargain Ledger: real filings and real prices. Each seat sees a different slice: the Quality Analyst gets six quarters of revenue and profit so it can see direction, not just a year-on-year number; the Value Analyst and Risk Officer get the month-by-month price path; the Risk Officer and the Skeptic also get each shortlisted company’s recent SEC-filed events, its last and next expected report dates, and the latest headlines from a public feed. The committee cannot browse beyond that, has no memory between sessions, and every analyst is Claude with a different job and a different instruction to disagree.

  • Quality Analyst judges the businesses: profitability, growth, debt, the three-year record.
  • Value Analyst judges the prices: cheap or dear against each company’s own past and its peers.
  • Risk Officer fits the picks to you: how much you can lose, how long you can wait, how concentrated it is.
  • The Skeptic argues against the whole thing, using the record: the top picks beat the index about half the time.
  • Macro Strategist brings the backdrop the others lack: the market’s trend, interest rates, the fear index, and which sectors have momentum and which are rolling over.
  • Two Chairs draft at the same time, one the consensus, one a materially different alternative; every draft is run through a stress test on real prices since 2020 (the 2022 bear market, the Covid crash, the worst twelve months), and the five seats choose which draft goes to the vote.
  • Adoption needs four of the five seats; a draft that names a stock no analyst examined is sent back; after two failed revisions the page says the committee did not adopt it.
  • No seat is shown the Ledger’s own score. The candidate list is every scored stock, in sector order, so the committee builds its own ranking each time; change the brief and the argument changes.

Running a session spends a small amount of your own Claude usage; the page asks once. Nothing you type is stored. And plainly: this is a study tool that shows you how a disciplined argument over real numbers looks. It is not investment advice, it does not know your taxes or your life, and the record it argues from is a coin flip per stock.
