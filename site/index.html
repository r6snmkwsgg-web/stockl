Strong companies · low price · sensible moment

Bargain Ledger

Large US companies ranked on three questions: does the business make real money, is the price below its own norm, and has it recently been marked down. Built from SEC filings and daily prices, scored in the open, and checked against fourteen years of history.

How the score works

Three questions, one number

Every company is asked the same three questions and scored 0 to 100 on each against the other companies in the list. The final score is a weighted mix of the three. Nothing here predicts the future: it ranks what is known today.

Quality

Is this a good business? Return on equity, how much revenue turns into free cash, revenue growth over the last year, the three-year share-price record against SPY, and whether the 200-day trend is rising. Debt above twice equity costs 15 points here.

Value

Is the price low? Price-to-earnings and price-to-sales compared with the company's own last five years, plus earnings yield and free-cash-flow yield compared with peers. Profit is capped at after-tax operating profit so one-off gains cannot make a stock look cheap.

Timing

Is now a sensible moment? How far the price sits below its 52-week high, whether that high was recent, whether the price is still near its 200-day average rather than in free fall, and a penalty for an over-bought RSI.

Red flags are listed on every card but do not change the score: losing money, negative free cash flow, shrinking revenue or profit, heavy debt, very high volatility, more than 20% below the 200-day average, a P/E above 50 or in the top fifth of its own history, profit boosted by one-off gains, stale filings, thin trading. An earlier version docked points for each flag; the record showed that made the picks worse, so now they are warnings for you to read, not arithmetic.

Why quality carries most of the weight. The first version of this score gave 40% to cheapness and 25% to the size of the pull-back. Tested honestly on 2012 to 2025, its top ten picks did worse than the average stock in the list. Only the quality questions ranked future returns, in both halves of the data; the cheap-and-dipping stocks were mostly cheap for a reason. The weights you see are what the evidence supports, and the section below shows both versions so you can judge for yourself.

Not scored means a hard rule failed: no profit or no free cash flow over the last twelve months, no new filing for about six months (135 trading days), price under $10, fewer than 500,000 shares traded a day, or filings without a share count. Banks, insurers and exchanges are judged on earnings, book value and return on equity instead of cash flow, which means nothing for a bank.

What it cannot do

Read this before acting on anything above

  • It cannot tell a temporary dip from a permanent one. A strong three-year record plus a 25% fall described Nvidia in early 2016 and Peloton in late 2021 equally well. The filings say what a company earned; they do not say why the price fell this month. Read the news before you read the score.
  • The list is partly hindsight. Half of it is today's largest companies, which by definition did well. The other half, companies that were once large and later fell, is there to keep the test honest, but businesses that went bankrupt are missing entirely.
  • It chases the last cycle. Rewarding recent profitability and a strong three-year record means the list can be full of last year's winners: oil companies in January 2023, Moderna and Meta in January 2022. A quality screen is partly a momentum screen, and it does not know when a cycle has ended.
  • Filings lag. The latest reported quarter can be four to five months old by the time the next one arrives. A profit collapse that happened last week is not in the numbers yet, though it usually is in the price, which is why the timing flags exist.
  • Earnings dates are not checked. A company about to report can move 10% overnight either way. Check the date yourself.
  • A high score is a starting point for research, not an instruction to buy. The record above shows the top picks lose to the index a large share of the time. This page is a study tool, not investment advice.
